# **STM32F405RGT6 WeAct SDIO, FatFs R0.12c Preallocation, and Industrial Storage Analysis**

Achieving reliable 4-bit SDIO logging on the WeAct Studio STM32F405RGT6 CoreBoard (WeActStudio.STM32F4\_64Pin\_CoreBoard) with FatFs R0.12c depends primarily on working around STM32CubeMX initialization bugs, STM32F405 silicon errata, and DMA memory-bus constraints rather than maximizing bus clock speed1. Available technical documentation contains no complete, board-specific verification table listing tested SD card models and measured D1–D3 pull-up resistor values solely for the WeAct STM32F405RGT6 revision; instead, working implementations span the shared WeAct 64-pin CoreBoard PCB (STM32F405/STM32F446) and ST Community STM32F405/STM32F4xx DMA production threads4. Across those engineering records, reliable 4-bit SDIO operation requires starting initialization in 1-bit mode before switching to 4-bit width, disabling SDIO hardware flow control and NEGEDGE clock dephasing per ST errata sheet ES0182, keeping all FatFs and DMA buffers out of the 64 KB Core Coupled Memory (0x10000000), and assigning SDIO\_IRQn a higher preemption priority than DMA and concurrent peripheral interrupts1.  
For a 1-hour, 44 MiB logging workload (46,137,344 bytes, or \~12.52 KiB/s sustained; decimal 44 MB is \~4.6% smaller), **10-minute chunked preallocation (6 × \~7.33 MiB) using f\_expand() outperforms a single 1-hour (44 MiB) preallocation** on power-loss containment, contiguous allocation reliability, and worst-case early-abort f\_truncate() overhead, while writing the exact same total payload volume10. No independent f\_truncate() millisecond benchmark on STM32F405 \+ FatFs R0.12c appears in the reviewed sources, but arithmetic on FAT32 allocation tables shows that premature logging termination forces f\_truncate() to clear up to 6× more FAT sectors (22–176 sectors across both FATs vs. 4–30 sectors for a 10-minute chunk)12, whereas full-run completion leaves the pointer at end-of-file where f\_truncate() performs no cluster freeing13.  
Because a 12.52 KiB/s stream uses less than 1% of the raw 1.5–3.0 MB/s bandwidth available in 1-bit SDIO mode (12–24 MHz)12, running in **1-bit SDIO mode** avoids D1–D3 signal-integrity and 4-bit initialization pitfalls entirely. Even in 1-bit mode, however, background flash garbage collection (GC) and busy signaling depend on the card's internal controller14. To minimize unpredictable write stalls, the strongest industrial-grade candidates verified across vendor datasheets and STM32 engineering threads are **Swissbit S-600u / S-250u** (true SLC) and **Swissbit S-56u** (3D pSLC with durabit™ sub-page firmware) or **SanDisk Industrial** (SDSDQAF3) microSD cards for the WeAct board's slot, and **MKDV** (MKDV1/2/4/8GIL-AST) or **CSNP4GCR01** SD NAND chips for custom soldered designs—whereas XTSD04G SD NAND has documented CTIMEOUT (STA=4) error reports on STM32 SDIO15.

## **1\. 4-Bit SDIO Implementations on the WeAct STM32F405RGT6 CoreBoard**

### **Board Pinout, Pull-Up Ambiguity, and Evidence Gaps**

WeAct Studio builds its STM32F405RGT6, STM32F412RET6, STM32F446RET6, and STM32F411RET6 CoreBoards around a shared 64-pin PCB design (WeActStudio.STM32F4\_64Pin\_CoreBoard)6. Both the Zephyr board documentation for the sibling weact/stm32f446\_core target and issue \#2 on WeAct's repository confirm the onboard microSD slot routing: PC8 (SDIO\_D0), PC9 (SDIO\_D1), PC10 (SDIO\_D2), PC11 (SDIO\_D3), PC12 (SDIO\_CK), and PD2 (SDIO\_CMD)5. Unlike WeAct's STM32H743 and STM32H750 CoreBoards—which route a mechanical card-detect (CD) switch to PD423—no dedicated card-detect GPIO is listed in the Zephyr or WeAct F4 64-pin documentation5.  
Publicly indexed text snippets of the WeAct V1.0 and V1.1 schematics (WeAct-STM32F4-64PIN-CoreBoard-V10-SchDoc and V11-SchDoc) confirm 3.3 V power conversion for the microSD socket and expose signal net names (PC8, PC9)27, but do not reveal whether external 10 kΩ–47 kΩ pull-up resistors are populated on D1–D3, D0, or CMD. Likewise, no standalone compatibility matrix of tested SD card brands exists specifically for the WeAct STM32F405RGT6 board. You should verify physical pull-ups directly in the PDF schematic inside the Hardware/ folder of the [WeActStudio GitHub repository](https://github.com/WeActStudio/WeActStudio.STM32F4_64Pin_CoreBoard) or measure resistance from PC8–PC11 and PD2 to 3.3 V6.  
Where low-cost STM32F4 boards omit external pull-up resistors—a documented flaw on the STM32F407VET6 Black Board—community solutions enable internal GPIO pull-ups (GPIO\_PULLUP) in CubeMX3. However, an STM32 SD NAND reference design notes that internal STM32 pull-ups exceed 50 kΩ and recommends external 10 kΩ pull-up resistors on CMD and data lines when internal pull-ups are too weak for fast bus edges30. In addition, SD cards ship with an internal card-side pull-up on DAT3 for card detection/SPI mode that must be disconnected via ACMD42 during 4-bit initialization31.

### **CubeMX, Clock, DMA, and Interrupt Configuration**

ControllersTech documents a working 4-bit SDIO \+ DMA \+ FatFs setup on the WeAct Studio STM32F446 board using the shared onboard microSD connector4, while ST Community thread 45595 and the dtiziano/stm32\_uSD\_SDIO4bit repository document working 4-bit DMA setups on STM32F405 and STM32F4xx CubeMX firmware3. On the WeAct STM32F405RGT6, an 8 MHz HSE crystal feeds the PLL to run the CPU at 168 MHz while generating the mandatory 48 MHz PLL48CLK (PLLQ tap) that drives both USB OTG FS and SDIO (SDIOCLK)12. With clock bypass disabled, SDIO\_CK \= 48 MHz / (CLKDIV \+ 2), giving 0.400 MHz during card identification (CLKDIV \= 118), 12.000 MHz at CLKDIV \= 2, and 24.000 MHz at CLKDIV \= 012.

| Parameter | Recommended CubeMX / HAL Setting | Engineering Basis & Source Caveats |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |

| Pin Mapping | PC8–PC11 (D0–D3), PC12 (CK), PD2 (CMD) | Verified on shared WeAct 64-pin PCB (stm32f446\_core / issue \#2)5 |

### **Tested SD Cards and 3.3 V UHS-I / SDXC Constraints**

Because no tested card list exists specifically for the WeAct STM32F405RGT6, compatibility expectations draw from broader STM32F4 SDIO deployments. An engineering selection guide (ampheo.com) cites **SanDisk Industrial 8 GB** as a proven STM32F407VGT6 4-bit SDIO \+ FatFs \+ DMA reference setup15, while an STM32F4 library write-up (mikrocontroller.bplaced.net) reports 4-bit SDIO working across 1 GB, 4 GB, and 16 GB SDHC cards alongside tested **Kingston 1 GB/2 GB** and **SanDisk 2 GB** microSD cards41. An ESP32 forum post cites 5–10 MB/s SDIO benchmarks on STM32F4 boards using a **32 GB SanDisk Extreme UHS-3 microSDHC** card42, and an ST Community thread (55503) confirms two **8 GB Class 10 SDHC** cards worked reliably in 1-bit mode under FatFs R0.12c on STM32F429I even when 4-bit mode failed43. A CSDN STM32F407 SDIO+DMA+FatFs case study also reports testing 32 cards across SanDisk, Samsung, Kingston, and Toshiba44.  
On STM32F405 boards, SDIO signals operate at 3.3 V logic27. Although UHS-I SD cards negotiate at 3.3 V initially before requesting a drop to 1.8 V on UHS-capable hosts (such as STM32H7 parts documented in RM0433)47, STM32F405 platforms remain capped at 3.3 V signaling (≤24 MHz without clock bypass)12. Furthermore, SDXC cards above 32 GB typically default to **exFAT**, whereas capacities up to 32 GB normally use **FAT32**50. Avoiding \>32 GB media lets you disable FF\_FS\_EXFAT (0) in FatFs R0.12c, eliminating C99 64-bit requirements, mandatory long-filename overhead, and R0.12c's exFAT bugs51.

### **Known STM32F405 \+ CubeMX \+ FatFs R0.12c Failure Modes**

Five recurring software and hardware defects account for most STM32F4 SDIO \+ FatFs R0.12c failures. First, **ST CubeMX bug 155983** generates hsd.Init.BusWide \= SDIO\_BUS\_WIDE\_4B inside MX\_SDIO\_SD\_Init(), causing HAL\_SD\_Init() to fail because SD cards always initialize in 1-bit mode3. You can preserve a clean CubeMX regeneration workflow by overriding the bus width in user code blocks:

> 1. Leave CubeMX configured for SD 4 bits Wide bus so all four data GPIOs (PC8–PC11) are initialized3.  
> 2. Inside USER CODE BEGIN SDIO\_Init 1 / 2 of sdio.c, override hsd.Init.BusWide \= SDIO\_BUS\_WIDE\_1B (or wrap the generated 4B block in \#if 0\) so HAL\_SD\_Init(\&hsd) runs in 1-bit mode3.  
> 3. Ensure BSP\_SD\_Init() switches the bus afterward by calling HAL\_SD\_ConfigWideBusOperation(\&hsd, SDIO\_BUS\_WIDE\_4B)3.  
> 4. If signals are routed externally or face reflections on a custom board, lower GPIO speed to Medium and verify 50 Ω series damping or external 10 kΩ pull-ups3.

Second, **DMA2 cannot access STM32F405 CCM RAM (0x10000000)**2. The STM32F405RGT6 splits its 192 KB SRAM into 128 KB general-purpose RAM and 64 KB Core Coupled Memory26. Placing the stack, FATFS/FIL sector buffers51, or caller write buffers in CCM causes DMA SDIO operations to stall after the first transfer or fail silently2.  
Third, **ST's generated sd\_diskio.c and bsp\_driver\_sd.c layers contain persistent driver defects**. ST Community threads across CubeFW F4 V1.18–V1.21+ document missing DMA/polling synchronization requiring patches to BSP\_SD\_WriteBlocks() and BSP\_SD\_ReadBlocks\_DMA()55, while a thread covering CubeMX 6.16.0 confirms ongoing DMA buffer-alignment handling bugs in sd\_diskio.c56. After HAL\_SD\_WriteBlocks(), firmware must spin until the card completes internal busy signaling (DAT0 busy state documented in SD datasheets), and developers must avoid viewing SDIO registers in a live debugger during active FIFO transfers16.  
Fourth, **interrupt preemption and FatFs R0.12c staleness (released March 2017\) degrade reliability**58. Because ES0182 requires disabling SDIO hardware flow control1, high-priority ISRs—such as ADC interrupts documented in STM32F405 thread 92588—can starve SDIO FIFO/DMA transfers and cause write failures8. Meanwhile, FatFs R0.12c suffers from a confirmed f\_unlink() bug that leaks clusters on fragmented exFAT volumes (fixed in R0.13a)52, and July 2026 security advisories covering pre-R0.16 FatFs (CVE-2026-6682, CVE-2026-6683, CVE-2026-6686, CVE-2026-6687) highlight integer overflows in mount\_volume(), exFAT f\_getlabel() stack overflows, and stale data exposure when extending files beyond EOF59. Keeping FAT32 (FF\_FS\_EXFAT \= 0\) and migrating ff.c/ff.h toward R0.15/R0.16 (elm-chan.org/fsw/ff/archives.html) mitigates these risks52.

## **2\. Preallocation Strategy: 1-Hour Full File vs. 10-Minute Chunks**

### **FAT32 Cluster and Sector Arithmetic**

At a 1-hour target of 44 MiB (46,137,344 bytes, or 12,815.93 B/s / 12.52 KiB/s), dividing the session into six 10-minute segments yields 7,689,557 bytes (\~7.33 MiB) per chunk12. Because FAT32 uses 4 bytes per cluster entry (128 entries per 512-byte sector) across two redundant File Allocation Tables (FAT1 and FAT2)12, cluster size directly determines the metadata sweep required during allocation and truncation:

| FAT32 Cluster Size | Full File (44 MiB) Clusters | Full File FAT Sectors (×2 FATs) | 10-Min Chunk (\~7.33 MiB) Clusters | 10-Min FAT Sectors (×2 FATs) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 4 KB | 11,264 | 88 (176 total) | 1,878 | 15 (30 total) |
| 8 KB | 5,632 | 44 (88 total) | 939 | 8 (16 total) |
| 16 KB | 2,816 | 22 (44 total) | 470 | 4 (8 total) |
| 32 KB | 1,408 | 11 (22 total) | 235 | 2 (4 total) |

Note: Figures calculated from binary 44 MiB (46,137,344 bytes) with ceiling division across 128 FAT32 entries per 512-byte sector12.

### **API Choice (f\_expand vs. f\_lseek) and f\_truncate() Latency**

FatFs R0.12c offers two preallocation mechanisms: f\_expand(fp, fsz, 1\) (introduced in R0.12 via FF\_USE\_EXPAND \= 1), which searches for and allocates a **single contiguous** area while keeping the read/write pointer at offset 010; and f\_lseek(fp, fsz), which allocates clusters that may be fragmented and advances the file pointer to fsz63. As shown in ST Community thread 41858 (which preallocates 3600 blocks via f\_expand(..., 1)) and the no-OS-FatFS logger guide, f\_expand() eliminates random write stalls caused by FAT scanning during logging65. Its primary failure mode is returning FR\_DENIED when no single contiguous hole of size fsz exists10. Securing six separate \~7.33 MiB contiguous regions succeeds far more reliably on an aging or partially filled volume than finding one 44 MiB contiguous extent10.  
No measured f\_truncate() execution-time benchmark on STM32F405 \+ FatFs R0.12c appears in the available documentation. However, FatFs specifies that f\_truncate(fp) shortens the file to the current file read/write pointer and has no effect if the pointer is already at end-of-file13. If a 1-hour run or a 10-minute chunk completes cleanly at its preallocated size, f\_truncate() performs zero cluster freeing in either strategy13. Conversely, if logging halts early—for instance, after 1 minute—a 44 MiB preallocation forces f\_truncate() to zero up to **6× more clusters and FAT sectors** (22–176 sectors across both FAT tables vs. 4–30 sectors for a 10-minute chunk)12. Because FatFs caches only a single 512-byte sector window inside the FATFS object51, walking up to 176 FAT sectors triggers repeated sector read/write cycles, magnifying the risk of hitting a card garbage-collection pause during an emergency shutdown.

### **Power-Loss Recovery and Filesystem Wear Trade-Offs**

Espressif's FatFs engineering documentation highlights that **FatFs is non-transactional**: an unexpected blackout during FAT or directory updates can corrupt chains or leave partitions damaged50. Preallocating via f\_expand() reduces mid-stream FAT modification because steady-state f\_write() calls fill pre-linked clusters without rewriting the FAT table at every cluster boundary10. Nevertheless, f\_expand(fp, fsz, 1\) leaves newly allocated cluster data undefined—a behavior noted in CVE-2026-6686 as exposing deleted prior data when files are extended beyond EOF—so an unclosed file after a blackout reports its full preallocated size (44 MiB or 7.33 MiB) with stale garbage past the last valid log record10.

| Evaluation Axis | Full-File (44 MiB / 1 Hour) | Chunked (6 × \~7.33 MiB / 10 Min) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Max unclosed data at risk | Up to **60 minutes** without periodic f\_sync()11 | Capped at **≤10 minutes** (completed chunks closed via f\_close())11 |
| Early-abort f\_truncate() scope | Up to 1,408–11,264 clusters (22–176 FAT sectors)12 | Bounded to 235–1,878 clusters (4–30 FAT sectors)12 |
| Contiguous f\_expand() failure risk | Higher FR\_DENIED risk as volume fills or fragments10 | 6× smaller contiguous extent needed per open10 |
| Post-blackout valid-data ambiguity | Entire 44 MiB tail undefined unless framed by app10 | Trailing ambiguity isolated to the single active ≤7.33 MiB file10 |
| Directory & FSINFO wear | 1 create \+ 1 close/hr (if zero f\_sync()); **high wear** if f\_sync() used often11 | 6 creates \+ 6 closes/hr; avoids frequent f\_sync() hot-sector rewrites11 |

At 12.52 KiB/s, both strategies write the exact same 44 MiB of log payload per hour, so flash wear differences arise strictly from **metadata write amplification**15. Leaving a 44 MiB file open for an hour without f\_sync() updates directory and FSINFO sectors only at open and close, but exposes an entire hour of buffered metadata to blackout loss11. Attempting to bound that loss on a single 1-hour file by calling f\_sync() every few seconds or minutes repeatedly rewrites the exact same 512-byte directory entry and FSINFO sectors11, amplifying FTL erase-block wear and triggering card garbage collection14.  
For FatFs R0.12c data logging, **10-minute chunked preallocation via f\_expand()** provides the strongest operational balance. Buffer samples in main non-CCM SRAM (128 KB general RAM2) and write aligned blocks (≥512 bytes, matching FAT cluster boundaries where RAM allows15), embed sequential frame headers or checksums in your stream so offline parsers can separate valid log bytes from undefined f\_expand() tail clusters after a blackout10, and perform f\_truncate() \+ f\_close() at every 10-minute boundary11. If f\_expand() fails with FR\_DENIED on a nearly full FAT32 card, fall back to deleting the oldest closed 10-minute segment (f\_unlink() works safely on FAT32, avoiding R0.12c's exFAT cluster-leak bug52) or extending via f\_lseek(). Where power-fail resilience outweighs FAT32 PC readability, consider pairing STM32 SD NAND hardware with **LittleFS** instead of FatFs30.

## **3\. Industrial-Grade SLC, pSLC, and SD NAND Options for 1-Bit SDIO**

### **What 1-Bit SDIO Solves—and Internal Card GC Limits**

Operating the STM32F405 SDIO peripheral in **1-bit mode (SDIO\_BUS\_WIDE\_1B)** routes only CLK (PC12), CMD (PD2), and DAT0 (PC8)5. It bypasses CubeMX's 4-bit HAL\_SD\_ConfigWideBusOperation() initialization defect (155983)3, eliminates D1–D3 pull-up and trace-skew vulnerabilities67, and retains 1.5 MB/s raw bus bandwidth at 12 MHz (3.0 MB/s at 24 MHz)12—more than **100×** the throughput required for 12.52 KiB/s logging12.  
Crucially, **bus width does not govern flash garbage collection**. Bad-block management, wear leveling, ECC, and GC run autonomously inside the storage device's embedded FTL controller14. Consumer MLC and TLC cards rely on complex multi-bit error correction and background folding that can trigger arbitrary GC pauses at any time14, stalling block writes via card busy signaling16. Industrial **true SLC** or **sub-page pSLC** architectures eliminate multi-level cell folding overhead, yielding far steadier write latency and higher endurance20.

### **Recommended MicroSD Cards and LGA-8 SD NAND Candidates**

No independent logic-analyzer benchmark measuring worst-case busy-timeout milliseconds across industrial SLC cards in STM32 1-bit SDIO mode was found in the reviewed literature, nor were STM32 SDIO test logs found for **Cactus** (220S/240S), **Transcend** (USD230I), or **Swissbit S-55u** (note that S-50u is a 3D TLC series20).
